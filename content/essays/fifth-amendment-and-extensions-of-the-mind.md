Recently, the government has made a move to charge a man for deleting evidence by providing a distress code rather than his real password to a cop. When the cop put the code in, it triggered GrapheneOS is to delete everything on the phone. 
https://www.404media.co/the-government-hopes-to-set-a-precedent-an-interview-with-the-man-charged-for-allegedly-wiping-his-grapheneos-phone/

Legal precedent is shaky on this. It's been ruled that you can't be forced to provide your password. But this isn't the same thing, the distress code was both a lie *and* a trick to get the cop to destroy evidence. Was providing the distress code destroying evidence directly? That's debatable, too. The cops are also allowed, in many circumstances, to force you to use biometrics to unlock your phone, break into a safe, search private possessions, etc.

But for encrypted data, should they? Hear me out. 

In a sense, is personal data 5th amendment-protected? Like, if it's encrypted and you've got the key in your brain, you don't have to incriminate yourself by telling them, there's precedent for that. Why not just go a step further and say that some protected files are an extension of the mind?

Like, isn't that kinda the core question we're asking, here? The encryption-secured (and distress code eraseable) cell phone fundamentally ties every bit of data in it to information stored in the brain. So, to plead the fifth on that key is to expand the protection against self-incrimination clause to all the information on the cell phone. This expansion of scope is *algorithmically* guaranteed, even if not always *legally* guaranteed.

But perhaps it should be legally guaranteed! It's not like other forms of information storage, there is a very real sense of ephemerality and expectation of privacy. As technology usage comes closer and closer to the brain itself, storing traces of increasingly intimate detail, we should really start considering where lies the line between the mind and its tools. If I've privately considered going out and tearing out flock cameras, for example, it's established that I don't have to admit that in court. But what if I've liked a couple posts about doing that, or just watched the whole thing and read the comments? Maybe posted an encouraging comment? Or saved a tutorial video? Or noticed a few mass surveillance cameras and noted down their street corners? These examples span from trace records of attention to concentrated organization of information. Crucially, they're all digital versions of things one might've done in their own brain if they didn't have a phone. They're things a person would likely *not* do digitally without encryption or at least an expectation of privacy. 

This will be increasingly important as we humans more deeply integrate ai into our lives. There's mounting evidence of its widespread usage for personal legal consultation, medical questions, emotional and mental health advice, and even in exploring spiritual questions. When it's a human performing those services, we grant protected status to both the human bearing the information as well as the associated physical and digital files. Attorney/client privilege, patient confidentiality, HIPAA, and clergy/penitent privilege all apply, even to physical records. Even mandated reporters must limit the information provided to authorities. I'd go so far as to suggest that we will eventually have to extend protected status to equivalent AI conversations, because the first principles of the situation remain the same.

The core thread through all of this is proximity to thought, to individuality. A physician or advocate's primary duty must be to the individual and not the state, and therefore the law must not be allowed to misalign those incentives. Communication between an individual and their therapist or pastor must be treated as equally outside the law as the individual's own private memories. These professionals are tasked with representing the individual's interests in good faith, effectively as extensions of the client or patient's own will. That's why the 5th amendment extends to the lawyer as well as to the lawyer's logs, HIPAA documents, therapist's notes, and so on. So I ask: Why not private, encrypted digital files, as well? They are, after all, on machines designed to subsume and extend existing functions of the mind itself. Personal data at least as intimate as a medical record. Increasingly complex software constructs that, like a doctor, cannot do their jobs if the user suspects any misaligned loyalties. Informed consent is regularly signed with memorized cryptographic keys.

_Fisher v. United States_ and _Andresen v. Maryland_ killed the old _Boyd_ "private papers" doctrine. When police seize documents you wrote voluntarily, nobody compelled you to say anything, so the Fifth Amendment simply isn't in play. But in light of modern technology, the line between private papers, priviledged records, and even the 5th amendment right to not self-incriminate is becoming fuzzy. More data will continue to be mined on every person, AI more deeply integrated, until the digital record simply reveals far more about our inner lives than the government should ever have access to.
