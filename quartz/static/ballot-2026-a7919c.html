Your ballot, today

Utah County general election, Tuesday Nov 3, 2026. Built for Alena by Jaden. Comes down tonight.

Where to go: Provo Health and Justice Building
151 S University Ave, Provo (the county building downtown). Open until 8 p.m. Get in line before 8 and they have to let you vote.

Open in Maps

You can register and vote there in one visit. Tell them you need to register and vote today. You'll fill out a provisional ballot, which doubles as your registration and gets counted once the county confirms you're eligible.

Bring:

  • Photo ID: driver license, state ID, or passport.
  • Proof you live here, if your ID shows a different address: anything with your name and this address, like a bank statement, pay stub, lease, utility bill, or government mail. A screenshot of an online statement on your phone may be accepted, but paper is safer.

Forgot something? Vote anyway. You'll have until next Monday to bring the missing ID to the county clerk.

The library won't work: Provo City Library has a drop box only, for people who already have a ballot. If the Health and Justice Building is inconvenient, Provo Legacy Village (4146 N University Ave) is the other Provo vote center, same hours and same rules.

Celeste Maloy R incumbent In Congress since 2023. Tax cuts, state control of land and water, pro-energy.

Core pitch

Former staffer for the congressman she replaced. Only Utahn on the House Appropriations Committee, which writes federal spending. Wants Utahns to "keep as much of their own money in their own pockets as possible." Supports nuclear power and light-touch AI rules; says data centers are a local decision.

🏠 Housing

Voted for the ROAD to Housing Act, which she says "cuts a lot of the red tape that drives up the cost of housing." It passed 358 to 32, so almost everyone voted for it.

Her affordability answer is tax cuts: no tax on tips or overtime, and keeping the 2017 tax cuts permanent.

🚌 Transit

No stated position found.

🌎 Immigration

Her votes, checked on the House roll calls:

  • Laken Riley Act (Jan 2025): Yes. Requires ICE to detain undocumented people who are charged with (not convicted of) theft, burglary, or shoplifting.
  • One Big Beautiful Bill Act (May and July 2025): Yes both times. Includes about $170 billion for immigration and border enforcement, including about $45 billion for immigrant detention.

Record

  • Missed 1.6% of votes (Nov 2023 to Jul 2026), about average.
  • The same Big Beautiful Bill also cut Medicaid and SNAP. The Congressional Budget Office estimated about 10 million more people uninsured by 2034.
  • Supported Trump's shrinking of Bears Ears and Grand Staircase national monuments.

Supporters say

She's experienced and sits on the committee that controls federal money, which matters for Utah's water and the Great Salt Lake. Tax cuts help families directly. Enforcing immigration law and detaining people arrested for crimes is basic public safety.

Critics say

She voted for a huge expansion of ICE detention, including people only charged with minor crimes, and for Medicaid cuts that hit working families. Udell says she sued to keep Utah's old congressional map.
Fact check: The Laken Riley Act does apply to people charged or arrested, not only convicted. Supporters and critics agree on that; they disagree on whether it's fair.

Utah House, District 62 (Provo & Springville)
The Legislature writes Utah's rules on zoning, renters, UTA, and in-state tuition. Probably the race that matters most for housing.
Norm Thurston RDavid Chappell D

State law decides what cities are allowed to require: parking minimums, lot sizes, how many roommates can share a house.

The Legislature decides things like in-state tuition for undocumented students and driving privilege cards.

The Legislature controls UTA's leadership and much of its money.

Norm Thurston R incumbent In the House since 2015. Economist. Tax cuts, health-cost bills, "local control" on zoning.

Core pitch

Princeton PhD economist and former state health-data official. Known for bills on drug prices, insulin access, and Utah's 0.05 DUI limit. Has voted for Utah's income tax cuts. On housing he favors local decisions: "the best solutions for Provo and Springville are not found in top-down mandates."

🏠 Housing

Says he supports policies that increase local housing supply. His votes on the contested housing bills mostly went the other way:

  • No on SB 181 (2025): stops cities from requiring garages on affordable owner-occupied homes and from oversized parking rules. Passed 50-20.
  • No on HB 492 (2026): state loans to cities for the pipes and roads that new housing needs. Passed 61-8.
  • No on HCR 6 (2026): support for Utah's housing plan. Passed 41-26.
  • No on HB 119 (2025): stops HOAs from banning solar panels outright.
🚌 Transit
  • No on SB 26 (2025): funding for housing and businesses built around train stations. He was one of only 5 no votes. (The bill also included a convention-center zone, which may be what he objected to.)
  • Yes on SB 197 (2026): reorganizes UTA so the governor appoints its director. Passed 65-2, so not a dividing line.
🌎 Immigration
  • No, twice, on HB 386 (2026) after it was rewritten to bar undocumented immigrants from in-state college tuition, some home-loan programs, and some professional licenses. It passed the House 39-33, unusually close, then died before the Senate voted. The sponsor was a Provo Republican; Thurston broke with her.
  • Yes on HB 136 (2026): police must impound the car of anyone driving without a license, permit, or driving privilege card (the card undocumented immigrants can get). Getting the car back costs a $600 fee plus towing, and someone with a valid license has to pick it up. Passed 55-12.
  • Yes on SB 31 (2025): created a state Refugee Services Office. Passed 67-7.

Other record

  • Voted to put Amendment B on this ballot (the 60% rule for tax initiatives), and for every contested bill tightening petition rules.
  • Declined to say how he'd vote on Amendments A and B.
  • Even Chappell credits his health-cost work (insulin access, drug-price transparency).

Supporters say

A serious policy economist with 11 years of experience who reads the bills. Cut taxes, lowered drug costs, and resisted his own party on the in-state tuition ban. Thinks Provo should decide Provo's zoning, not Salt Lake.

Critics say

Voted against the state's main tools to make housing cheaper (parking rules, housing infrastructure, transit-area housing). Voted to make citizen initiatives harder. Backed the car impound law that hits immigrant drivers hard.

David Chappell D BYU civil engineer, first run. Renter protections, small-scale housing, abolish ICE.

Core pitch

Co-founded BYU's Cougar Pride Center. Running on cost of living: cut sales tax on groceries and essentials, free school meals, renter and worker protections. Says his plan has no tax increases. Opposes Amendment B. Refuses corporate money.

🏠 Housing
  • Low-interest state loans so regular homeowners can add a unit (an attic, a backyard cottage) and sell it.
  • Make it easier to turn houses into duplexes. Replace limits on how many unrelated people can share a house with health-and-safety standards.
  • Not high-rises: "My plan is about small additions, not high-rises." Against "lifeless townhomes" built by big developers.
  • Renters: ban application fees, cap deposits at 2 months' rent, give a week instead of 3 days to move after an eviction notice, keep first evictions off your record, and require landlords to justify rent increases, with a state office to appeal to.
🚌 Transit

Nothing in his platform. His site argues the state's recent income tax cuts could have paid for fare-free UTA (buses, TRAX, FrontRunner) many times over, but he doesn't promise it.

🌎 Immigration

"Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) should be abolished." Says immigration violations are civil matters for civil courts, and "I will do everything in my power as a state legislator to protect Utahns from ICE."

Fact check: He says "ICE has killed hundreds of people since it was created in 2003." More than 200 people have died in ICE custody since 2003 by ICE's own reports, but most deaths are recorded as medical causes or suicide; critics blame poor care. "Killed" is his framing. Also, a state legislator can't abolish ICE (that's Congress). He could limit how much Utah helps it.

Record

No voting record; he's never held office.

Other: says data centers "should not be built in Utah." Wants ranked-choice voting and an independent commission to draw maps.

Supporters say

The most detailed renter-protection plan on the ballot. Lets ordinary homeowners build, not just big developers. Would protect citizen initiatives and immigrant neighbors.

Critics say

Making landlords justify rent increases is a step toward rent control, which economists mostly find shrinks the rental supply over time. Opposing high-rises and townhomes limits how much housing gets built. "Abolish ICE" is a position most Utah voters reject. No experience.

Amendment B: 60% to pass tax-raising initiatives
Should citizen-written laws that raise taxes need 60% of the vote instead of a simple majority? The contested measure on the ballot.
ForAgainst
What's an initiative?

A law that regular people write, gather signatures for, and put on the ballot themselves, without the Legislature. In 2018 Utah voters used one to expand Medicaid (it passed with about 53%) after the Legislature wouldn't.

What it does: any citizen initiative that creates a tax, raises one, or slows a scheduled tax cut would need 60% to pass. It doesn't apply to the Legislature, which can still raise taxes with a simple majority.

Matters for you because initiatives that pay for something (health coverage, transit, housing funds) usually need a tax to do it.

Supporters say

Raising your neighbors' taxes should take broad agreement. Initiatives skip the vetting and budget tradeoffs that bills go through. Voters still decide; the bar is just higher. (Sen. Lincoln Fillmore, Utah Taxpayers Association, Americans for Prosperity)

Opponents say

It's the Legislature's latest attempt to weaken initiatives after voters' power was upheld in 2024. Medicaid expansion would have failed under it. And it applies retroactively to Nov 1, 2026. (Better Utah, Better Boundaries, Utah Forward Party)
Fact check: True that no 2018 initiative got 60%, so Medicaid expansion and medical marijuana both would have failed. True that the Legislature isn't held to the same bar.

Amendment A: how amendments get announced
Replaces the rule that proposed amendments be printed in newspapers with 60 days of public notice set by state law. Procedural.
ForAgainst

Two amendments were thrown off the 2024 ballot partly because the state didn't meet the newspaper rule. A 2025 law makes initiatives follow the same rule, so if A fails, initiative backers would have to pay roughly $1.4 million for newspaper notices.

Supporters say

Newspapers aren't how people get news anymore. Saves up to $1.4 million every two years and spares initiative campaigns that cost. Passed the Legislature unanimously, Democrats included.

Concerns

No organized opposition. The worry: the Constitution would only say "60 days, in a manner established by state law," so the Legislature could weaken the notice later without a public vote.

Jaden's pick

For.

It quietly helps initiatives by killing a $1.4 million bill they'd otherwise face. The risk of the Legislature weakening notice later is real but smaller.

Why Pohlman is in the news

She joined the unanimous rulings that stopped the Legislature from overriding voter initiatives and that kept its initiative-weakening Amendment D off the 2024 ballot. The state Republican Party chair said she has "zero business being in the robe," and Gov. Cox said unhappy voters should vote against those judges.

The other side: the Utah State Bar says "disagreement with a specific ruling should not be the basis for removal." No Utah justice has lost a retention vote since 1990.

Low and Howell
